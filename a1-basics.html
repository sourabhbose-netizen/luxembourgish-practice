2. Verbs — Present Tense

What is an infinitive? The "base form" of a verb — like "to work", "to eat", "to go" in English. In Luxembourgish, infinitives always end in -en (schaffen, iessen, goen).

What is conjugation? Changing the verb to match who is doing it. English: I work, he works. Luxembourgish does the same but with different endings.

Regular Verbs — The Pattern

How regular verbs work: Take the infinitive (e.g. schaffen), remove the -en ending to get the root (schaff), then add these endings:
ech: -en | du: -s | hien: -t | mir: -en | dir: -t | si: -en

3. Sentence Structure

The #1 Rule: The verb ALWAYS goes in position 2 in a Luxembourgish sentence. Always. No exceptions.

Think of it like a train: Position 1 = who/what/when, Position 2 = VERB (always here!), Position 3+ = the rest.

Normal Sentence

Position 1 (Subject)Position 2 (VERB)Rest
Echschaffenbei Amazon.
Meng Frakachtgutt.
Ech schaffen bei Amazon. = I work at Amazon.

Inversion (when you start with time/place)

What is inversion? When you start a sentence with a time word (yesterday, tomorrow, today), the verb STAYS in position 2, so the subject moves to position 3. The verb and subject "swap" places.
Position 1 (Time)Position 2 (VERB)Position 3 (Subject)Rest
Gëschterhunnechgeschafft.
Muerginnechan Indien.
De Weekendsimirgaangen.
Normal: Ech hunn gëschter geschafft.
Inversion: Gëschter hunn ech geschafft.
Both mean: I worked yesterday. (Same meaning, just different emphasis!)

4. Past Tense (Perfekt)

What is a past participle? It's the form of a verb used to describe something that already happened.
In English: work → worked, eat → eaten, go → gone.
In Luxembourgish: schaffen → geschafft, iessen → giess, goen → gaangen.

How to build the past tense:
Subject + hunn or sinn (helper verb) + rest of sentence + past participle at the END

Which helper?
- hunn = for most verbs (actions you do: work, eat, cook, watch)
- sinn = for movement verbs (your body goes from A to B: go, come, fly, drive)

Regular past participle rule: GE + root + T
schaffen → ge + schaff + t = geschafft

Ech hunn gëschter mat menger Fra am Restaurant giess.

5. Future Tense

How to say "I'm going to...": Use the verb goen (to go) conjugated in present + put the action verb at the END in infinitive form.

Think of it like English "I'm going to work" = Ech ginn schaffen.
PronounGoen conjugatedExampleEnglish
echginnEch ginn muer schaffen.I'm going to work tomorrow.
dugeesDu gees kachen.You're going to cook.
hien/sigeetHien geet reesen.He's going to travel.
mirginnMir ginn iessen.We're going to eat.
dir/DirgittGitt Dir kommen?Are you going to come?
siginnSi ginn an Indien.They're going to India.
Structure: Subject + ginn/gees/geet + rest + infinitive at END
Ech ginn muer mat menger Fra iessen. = I'm going to eat with my wife tomorrow.

6. Negation (Saying "not" or "no")

Two ways to say "no":
- net = "not" — use before verbs/adjectives
- keen/keng = "no" / "not a" — use before nouns

Think of it like English:
"I do not work" = net
"I have no car" = keen
RuleExampleEnglish
net = notEch schaffen net.I don't work.
net in past tense (before participle)Ech hunn net geschafft.I didn't work.
keen = no (male noun)Ech hunn keen Auto.I have no car.
keng = no (female/plural noun)Ech hunn keng Geschwëster.I have no siblings.
näischt = nothingEch hunn näischt gemaach.I did nothing.
ni = neverEch ginn ni an de Casino.I never go to the casino.

7. Articles (the, a/an)

What are articles? Little words before nouns: "the" (definite) and "a/an" (indefinite).
In Luxembourgish, they change based on the gender of the noun (male, female, or neutral).

How do I know the gender? You mostly have to learn it with the noun. Some rules help (see class notes on genders), but mostly: memorize!

Definite Article = "the"

Male (m.)Female (f.)Neutral (n.)Plural
de(n)d'd'd'
den Auto (the car)d'Fra (the woman)d'Haus (the house)d'Kanner (the children)

Indefinite Article = "a / an"

Male (m.)Female (f.)Neutral (n.)Plural
e(n)enge(n)— (no article)
en Auto (a car)eng Fra (a woman)en Haus (a house)Kanner (children)
When to use de(n) vs de? Use den when the next word starts with: d, n, t, z, ts, or a vowel. Otherwise use de.
Same rule for e(n): use en before d, n, t, z, ts, vowels.

8. Dativ (after prepositions)

What is the Dativ? It's a grammatical case that changes "the" and "a" when they come after certain prepositions (words like "with", "from", "at").

In plain English: After the words mat, vun, no, aus, zënter, während, bei, zu, the articles change shape.

Think of it like: "the car" = den Auto, but "with the car" = mat dem Auto. The word "the" changes because of "with".
